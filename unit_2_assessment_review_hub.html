1. VSEPR 3D Shapes

How Lone Pairs Push Bonds Down

Double and triple bonds count as 1 bonding domain. Unshared lone pairs on the central atom take up extra space and push bonding pairs closer together:

Bonds Lone Pairs 3D Shape Examples
20LinearCO₂, CS₂, HCN
21 or 2BentSO₂ (1 LP), H₂O, H₂S (2 LP)
30Trigonal PlanarCH₂O, BF₃, SO₃
31Trigonal PyramidalNH₃, PF₃, PCl₃
40TetrahedralCH₄, CF₄, CH₂Cl₂, CH₃Cl
⚠️ #1 Class Misconception Alert (CO₂): Carbon in CO₂ (O=C=O) has ZERO lone pairs on the central carbon! It is Linear & Nonpolar (the two opposite C=O bond dipoles cancel out), NOT bent!
2. Polarity & Symmetry

When Do Bond Dipoles Cancel Out?

  • Nonpolar Molecule (Dipoles Cancel): The 3D geometry is completely symmetrical AND all outer atoms are identical (CO₂, CF₄, CCl₄, CH₄, BF₃).
  • Polar Molecule (Dipoles Do NOT Cancel):
    • Has a lone pair on the central atom creating an uneven electron distribution (NH₃, PF₃, H₂O, H₂S, SO₂), OR
    • Has different outer atoms attached to the central atom (CH₃Cl, CHF₃, CH₂Cl₂, HCN, CH₂O).
⚠️ The CH₂Cl₂ 2D Drawing Trap: Even if CH₂Cl₂ is drawn on flat paper with Cl atoms across from each other, its real 3D shape is tetrahedral! In a 3D tetrahedron, the two C–Cl dipoles point toward one side and do NOT cancel — so CH₂Cl₂ is POLAR!
3. IMFs, Isomers & Detergents

Intermolecular Forces & Solubility Rules

1. Dispersion (LDF)
All molecules (only force in nonpolar). Stronger with more e⁻ / larger size.
2. Dipole-Dipole
Between polar molecules (CH₃Cl, H₂S, CH₂Cl₂).
3. Hydrogen Bond
Strongest! Needs H directly bonded to N, O, or F (H₂O, NH₃, HF, R-OH).

Structural Isomers: Same molecular formula (e.g., C₂H₆O for ethanol CH₃CH₂OH and dimethyl ether CH₃OCH₃) but different atom connectivity and different physical properties (ethanol has an O–H bond for hydrogen bonding, so its boiling point is much higher!).

Surfactants (Soaps & Detergents): Have a polar head (attracted to polar water) and a nonpolar hydrocarbon tail (attracted to nonpolar oil/grease), bridging oil and water so grease washes away.

4. Ionic Solids & Coulomb's Law

Lattice Energy, Conductivity & Brittleness

  • Coulomb's Law (F ∝ (q₁·q₂) / r²):
    • Bigger Ion Charges = Much Stronger Attraction: CaO / MgO (+2 / −2) have much higher melting points than LiF / NaCl (+1 / −1).
    • Smaller Ion Radius = Closer Distance = Stronger Attraction: When charges are equal (+1/−1), NaCl melts higher than NaI because Cl⁻ is smaller than I⁻, so the ions are closer together.
  • Electrical Conductivity: Requires mobile charged particles (ions). Solid KBr does NOT conduct because ions are locked in a rigid crystal lattice; molten (liquid) or aqueous KBr DOES conduct because ions (NOT free electrons!) are free to move.
  • Why Ionic Crystals Shatter (Brittleness): Striking a crystal shifts a layer of ions so like charges (+/+ and −/−) align next to each other, causing strong electrostatic repulsion that cleaves the crystal.

Periodic Table of the Elements

Color-coded by element classification (Metals, Transition Metals, Metalloids, Nonmetals, and Noble Gases).

Main-Group Metals Transition Metals Metalloids Nonmetals Noble Gases
How to Determine Valence Electrons & Ion Charges by Group:
Group 1 (Alkali Metals)
1 valence electron → loses 1 e⁻ to form a +1 ionic charge.
Group 2 (Alkaline Earth Metals)
2 valence electrons → loses 2 e⁻ to form a +2 ionic charge.
Groups 3–12 (Transition Metals)
Form cations with variable positive charges shown by Roman numerals — e.g., (I) = +1, (II) = +2, (III) = +3.
Group 13
3 valence electrons → metals in this group lose 3 e⁻ to form a +3 ionic charge.
Group 14
4 valence electrons → typically shares 4 covalent bonds to complete an octet.
Group 15
5 valence electrons → nonmetals gain 3 e⁻ to form a −3 ionic charge.
Group 16
6 valence electrons → nonmetals gain 2 e⁻ to form a −2 ionic charge.
Group 17 (Halogens)
7 valence electrons → nonmetals gain 1 e⁻ to form a −1 ionic charge.
Group 18 (Noble Gases)
8 valence electrons (2 for He) → full outer shell, 0 ionic charge.
